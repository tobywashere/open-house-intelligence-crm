# B2B SaaS Deal Desk and Procurement Reference

> **Illustrative sample.** This document was written to demonstrate the structure of a knowledge pack and to exercise retrieval. It is not researched industry guidance — replace it with your own material before relying on it.

## Security Review and Compliance Gates

### SOC 2 Type II as a Purchase Gate

Most mid-market and enterprise buyers now require a SOC 2 Type II report before a contract reaches legal review, not after. A Type I report attests that controls are designed correctly at a point in time; Type II attests that they operated effectively over an observation window, typically three to twelve months. Buyers with their own compliance obligations generally reject Type I for anything touching customer data.

Practical effect on a deal: attach the security packet — SOC 2 report under NDA, subprocessor list, data-flow diagram, and penetration-test summary — to the first substantive follow-up rather than waiting for the buyer to ask. Deals that surface a compliance gap during procurement typically slip by a full quarter, because the remediation cycle and the buyer's next budget window rarely align.

### Data Residency and Subprocessors

Buyers operating under GDPR, or in regulated sectors, increasingly require that customer data remain in a named region and that any subprocessor change come with advance notice. A vendor without regional hosting options is often eliminated before the technical evaluation begins. When a prospect asks early about residency, treat it as a qualifying signal rather than a detail: it usually means their legal team is already involved.

### Penetration Testing and Vulnerability Disclosure

Enterprise security questionnaires commonly ask for an annual third-party penetration test, a documented vulnerability disclosure policy, and a stated remediation SLA by severity. Buyers rarely read the full report; they check that one exists, that critical findings were closed, and that the retest confirms it.

## Procurement Mechanics

### The Deal Desk Function

Larger sellers route non-standard terms through a deal desk — a cross-functional group covering finance, legal, and revenue operations that approves discounts, payment terms, and contract deviations. Understanding whether a prospect's own organization has an equivalent function matters: if it does, the person you are talking to may not control timing, and the real constraint is the deal desk's review cadence.

### Multi-Year Discounting and Ramp Deals

Multi-year commitments are typically discounted 10–20% against the annual list price, with the discount tied to payment terms as much as duration. A ramp deal — lower fees in year one that step up as adoption grows — often closes when a flat multi-year price would not, because it matches the buyer's realistic rollout schedule. The trade is forecast complexity: ramp revenue is harder to model and creates a visible renewal cliff.

### Payment Terms as a Negotiation Lever

Net-30 versus net-60 is frequently worth more to a seller's cash position than a small discount is worth to the buyer's budget. When a buyer pushes on price late in a cycle, offering annual prepayment in exchange for the requested discount often resolves it without eroding the effective rate.

### Legal Redlines That Predict Slippage

Certain redlines reliably signal a longer cycle: uncapped liability, unlimited indemnification, source-code escrow, and custom SLAs with financial penalties. Each typically requires sign-off outside the buying team. When two or more appear in the same redline, treat the stated close date as optimistic.

## Buying Committee Dynamics

### Buying Committee Roles: Champion, Economic Buyer, and Blocker

A functioning deal has an identified champion who wants the outcome, an economic buyer who controls the budget, and — nearly always — a blocker whose objection has not yet surfaced. Deals stall most often not because the champion loses interest but because the economic buyer was never engaged directly, leaving the champion to sell internally without support.

### Buying Committee Size and Cycle Length

Buying committees grow with contract value. Below roughly $25,000 annual value, one or two people typically decide. Between $25,000 and $100,000, expect three to five including a security reviewer. Above $100,000, expect procurement, legal, security, and an executive sponsor, and plan for a cycle measured in quarters rather than weeks.

### Vendor Consolidation Pressure

When budgets flatten, buyers consolidate overlapping tools rather than cutting categories outright. This favors platform breadth over point-solution depth in first conversations: the question shifts from "is this the best tool for the job" to "how many existing line items does this replace."

## Renewals and Expansion

### Usage Signals That Predict Churn

Declining weekly active usage, a departed champion, and an unrenewed executive sponsor are the three strongest churn predictors. Any one of them warrants an intervention conversation a full quarter before renewal, not in the renewal month.

### Expansion Timing

Expansion lands best when tied to a customer milestone — a new team, a new region, a funding event — rather than to the seller's quarter end. Attempting expansion at the same time as a renegotiated renewal usually produces neither.
