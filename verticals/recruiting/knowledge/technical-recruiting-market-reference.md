# Technical Recruiting Market Reference

> **Illustrative sample.** This document was written to demonstrate the structure of a knowledge pack and to exercise retrieval. It is not researched industry guidance — replace it with your own material before relying on it.

## Compensation Structure

### Base, Bonus, and Equity

Technical compensation is usually quoted as total compensation across three components: base salary, target bonus, and equity valued at grant. Candidates compare offers on total compensation; finance teams budget on base. Quoting one number without breaking it down is the most common source of late-stage offer disputes.

### Equity Vesting and Refresh Grants

The standard grant vests over four years with a one-year cliff, after which vesting continues monthly or quarterly. The number candidates rarely ask about, and should, is the refresh grant: what they receive in year two and beyond. An initial grant that looks generous against a competitor's can be worth substantially less over four years if the competitor refreshes annually and the offering company does not.

### Compensation Bands and Levelling

Employers set bands by level and location, typically with a midpoint and a range of roughly plus or minus fifteen percent. Offers above midpoint usually require justification and an extra approval. This is why levelling matters more than negotiation: moving a candidate up a level changes the band entirely, while negotiating within a band moves the number by a few percent.

### Location-Adjusted Pay

Remote-friendly employers commonly tie compensation to the candidate's location using published geographic tiers. Candidates relocating from a higher-cost to a lower-cost market are sometimes subject to adjustment at the move, which is a material detail worth surfacing before the offer rather than after.

## Candidate Motivation and Offer Acceptance

### What Senior Candidates Optimize For

Senior individual contributors and engineering leaders consistently rank scope, technical ownership, and the quality of the people they will work with above compensation, provided compensation is competitive. Leading a senior conversation with the tech stack rather than the problem space is a common mismatch: the stack is a filter, not a motivator.

### Counteroffers

A meaningful share of accepted offers are followed by a counteroffer from the candidate's current employer. Counteroffers are most effective when the candidate's stated reason for leaving is compensation, and least effective when it is scope or management. Establishing the real reason early — and testing whether money would actually fix it — predicts whether an offer will survive.

### Notice Periods

Two weeks is standard in North America. Longer notice is common in the United Kingdom and much of Europe, where one to three months is normal and is contractual rather than customary. Start dates that ignore this create avoidable friction with the hiring manager's planning.

## Interview Process Design

### Process Length and Drop-Off

Candidate drop-off rises sharply with the number of interview stages and the elapsed time between them. Processes that stretch past three weeks from first contact to offer lose candidates to faster-moving employers regardless of the eventual offer's quality. Compressing scheduling is usually cheaper than raising compensation.

### Take-Home Exercises

Take-home assignments reduce interview time but increase drop-off, particularly among senior candidates and those currently employed. When used, they are best kept under two hours and paired with a clear statement of how the work will be evaluated.

### Structured Debriefs

Unstructured debriefs favor the loudest interviewer. Written independent feedback submitted before the group discussion measurably reduces that effect and produces decisions that hold up when challenged.

## Sourcing and Market Signals

### Passive Candidate Outreach

Response rates to passive outreach depend far more on specificity than on volume. A message naming the actual problem the team is solving, and why this candidate specifically, outperforms templated outreach by a wide margin. Employers experiencing public change — a return-to-office mandate, a reorganization, a funding event — produce short windows where passive candidates respond at unusually high rates.

### Layoffs and Hiring Freezes

Layoffs at a large employer briefly increase available senior talent in that market, but the effect is short-lived: strong candidates from well-known companies typically clear the market within weeks. Speed of process matters more during these windows than at any other time.

### Immigration and Work Authorization

Work-permit timelines and sponsorship policy shape which candidates are realistically available. Confirming authorization and any sponsorship need before the technical loop avoids the worst outcome in recruiting — a candidate who clears every stage and cannot be hired.

## Pipeline Health

### Ratios Worth Tracking

Screen-to-onsite and onsite-to-offer ratios diagnose different problems. A weak screen-to-onsite ratio usually means sourcing is off-target. A weak onsite-to-offer ratio usually means the screen is not testing what the loop tests. Offer-to-accept measures whether compensation and role framing match the market.

### Time-to-Fill Versus Quality

Time-to-fill is easy to measure and easy to game by lowering the bar. Tracking it alongside first-year retention keeps the tradeoff visible.
